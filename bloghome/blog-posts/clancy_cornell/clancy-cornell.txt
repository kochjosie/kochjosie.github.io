In the wake of Lindsay Clancy and the Cornell Seven case, social media has ramped up its already absurd levels of extremity on the topic of gender. While I usually use the web version of Instagram, I used the app this past week, and I have a few reflections on what I saw.


Lindsay Clancy was a mother to three young children, all of whom she murdered in a state of post-partum psychosis. Like most people who don’t spend too much time on social media, this story broke my heart; it did not occur to me how this story could relate to either feminism or the manosphere in any way. Parents killing their children, spouses, and themselves is an unfortunate event that happens with relative regularity. These are usually psychotic episodes where one believes themselves to be a savior to their family when, in reality, they are causing their end. I have no knowledge on the gender breakdown of these events, but anecdotally I do not see mothers committing these crimes more than fathers. My only real takeaway was that sometimes people have psychotic episodes and do horrible things during them. That’s just the imperfect world we live in, and I’m not sure that we’ll ever completely rid ourselves of that.


However, I did not consume this news in any kind of normal world. I was on Instagram. I found the public’s reaction to the case deeply bizarre. On the one hand, men were using this as a proof to say that mothers do not love their babies and that women should have no rights. On the other hand, women were arguing that Clancy was an innocent victim and conflating her post-partum psychosis with their post-partum depression through the ‘Same, Lindsay’ trend. These reactions seemed to me to be deeply insensitive to the nature of what had happened. I was confused why people were making this story about them.


The ‘Cornell Seven’ case involved a young woman who was gang raped at a fraternity house by seven young men. The young woman was pressured into taking drugs to a point of extreme intoxication, and her body was advertised over Signal as being free to rape. While this case is more obviously tied to feminism and gender relations, I did not see it being talked about in any kind of productive way. I live in a college town. It is no secret that fraternities and their members are not exactly friendly toward women, committing crimes or not. I thought that perhaps this horrific story would spur a productive conversation about Greek Life’s relationship with toxic masculinity. This kind of behavior is unfortunately common in large groups of young men who are often committed to some social cause. In these kinds of settings, men commit increasingly aggressive and violent actions in order to prove themselves as masculine to the group. The pursuit of being viewed as masculine trumps the desire to do what is right. This can be seen around the world and across history, particularly when viewing the actions of armies or militia made up of young, disaffected men. There is also a sense of anonymity that occurs in any group setting. Individuals feel that they are acting as part of a herd rather than feeling responsible for their own actions. These are all well-documented psychological concepts. Instead, I saw men claiming, without evidence, that Jane Doe was lying (because women always lie) and women claiming that this kind of violence was typical of the average man. In both the case of Clancy and Cornell, horrible cases of violence were being used as ammunition to pursue personal causes.


What I found most interesting is that changing my content recommendation settings only seemed to worsen the issue. I turned off political and sensitive recommendations. I blocked around a hundred terms relating to gender and the culture war in both comments and content. Neither of these actions worked. In fact, I noticed that I was being shown even more gender-war content, just with an added ‘Are you interested?’ button. Unsurprisingly, hitting ‘no’ yielded no results.


With all this, I’d like to return to my initial question— who exactly is the gender war benefitting? I don’t believe it benefits men or women. While I’ve seen many people comment on how this hurts romantic endeavors, I haven’t seen many comment on the disastrous effect it has on non-romantic interactions. On an average day, the average person interacts with an even mix of men and women. What kinds of effects are left on the psyche of someone who believes that half the people they interact with on a daily basis are their enemy solely by nature of their gender? Needless to say, I don’t think the effects are good. So… who is benefiting from this? I don’t have a firm answer, but my instinct is to say the answer lies in the same reason that boys and girls toys are often split at big box stores.


First, the obvious point: social media companies benefit from culture war content. It’s emotionally engaging and takes little intellectual effort, so people stay on the apps longer and see more ads. But I don’t think that’s the whole story. I specify Meta because I haven’t seen this level of extremity anywhere outside of Instagram Reels. I don’t think the goal is short-term profit. I think it’s long-term: a population of men and women divided against each other is easier to manipulate, as customers and as voters. Gender is just a convenient line to divide us along. I can’t prove this, but the fact that the content got worse when I actively tried to avoid it makes it hard to believe this is only about clicks.


Just the other night, Shane Gillis’s performance on SNL has been the new topic of division. Perhaps unsurprisingly, I’ve removed the app from my phone and have returned to my much less interesting www.instagram.com once more.
